Why Determinism and Provenance Are Required for Domain-Gap Evaluation

In the evaluation of domain gaps within the `ultimate_pipeline`, determinism and provenance are foundational requirements rather than implementation details. The objective of the pipeline is to compare manually authored CARLA maps with maps generated through an OpenStreetMap–to–OpenDRIVE conversion process. Such a comparison is only scientifically meaningful if observed differences can be attributed to the map representations themselves, rather than to uncontrolled variation in the pipeline that produces them.

Determinism ensures that identical inputs yield identical outputs across repeated executions of the pipeline. In the context of autonomous driving simulation using CARLA 0.9.16 on Windows, non-deterministic behavior introduces ambiguity into experimental results. If the same OpenStreetMap extract produces different OpenDRIVE files or simulation behavior across runs, it becomes impossible to determine whether observed differences arise from the domain gap under investigation or from incidental variability in the processing steps. Determinism therefore serves as a prerequisite for isolating causal effects and for enabling repeatable experiments.

Provenance complements determinism by providing traceability across the transformation stages of the pipeline. The `ultimate_pipeline` performs multiple data transformations, from geographic OpenStreetMap data to OpenDRIVE road networks and finally to CARLA-compatible simulation maps. Without explicit provenance information, discrepancies between the source data and the simulator output cannot be reliably traced back to specific processing stages. Provenance allows each artifact to be associated with its originating inputs, transformation parameters, and intermediate outputs, making it possible to identify where and how domain discrepancies are introduced.

An unstable pipeline fundamentally undermines the validity of quantitative evaluation. When failures or inconsistencies occur unpredictably, they introduce variance that is unrelated to the system being measured. In such cases, numerical results may reflect properties of the pipeline itself rather than properties of the simulated environment or the domain gap being studied. This confounds interpretation and renders quantitative comparisons unreliable, as measurements can no longer be assumed to correspond to a fixed experimental setup.

Failure logging plays a critical role in restoring scientific validity under these conditions. Rather than discarding failures as unusable data, the `ultimate_pipeline` treats them as structured experimental outcomes. By recording failures deterministically, including the conditions under which they occur, the pipeline transforms instability into observable signal. Simulator crashes, invalid map constructs, or pipeline interruptions become measurable indicators of domain mismatch or processing fragility. This approach enables reproducibility, as failures can be re-examined under controlled conditions, and supports systematic improvement of both the pipeline and the evaluation methodology.

In summary, determinism constrains experimental variability, provenance enables causal tracing of discrepancies, and structured failure logging ensures that instability contributes to, rather than undermines, scientific evaluation of domain gaps within the `ultimate_pipeline`.

## Map Events Log

The pipeline writes a structured `map_events.jsonl` log under each run’s artifacts directory. Each record captures what changed in the map (road/lane identifiers, s-range, removed length) along with the final map hash. This supports domain-gap claims by enabling precise attribution of *where* geometry changed, *how much* was removed or masked, and whether simulator failures occurred before or after those changes. The log is append-only and can be correlated with quality-gate outputs and CARLA load checks for reproducible evidence.
